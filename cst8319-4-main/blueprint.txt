Learn Screen (lib/screens/learn_screen.dart)

Screen Title: LEARN
Screen Headline: The EFFT framework, explained.
Screen Body: Tap any topic to read about it. Each section is a short, focused read.

Topic 1: The EFFT Framework
Subtitle: Why validation comes first
Section 1 Heading: A small shift can change the moment
Section 1 Body: When children are stressed or distressed, their ability to think clearly and problem-solve becomes limited. Connection and calm have to come first before anything else can move forward.
Section 1 Callout: Validation + Support is a quick, learnable framework that helps bring your child's brain to a more flexible state.
Section 2 Heading: What caregivers gain
Section 2 Body: When you validate before you fix or reassure, something shifts for your child and for you.
Section 2 Bullets:
Less arguing, shutdown, and refusal when things are hard
More flexibility and cooperation over time
More confidence handling big emotions, yours and theirs
A stronger sense of connection, even in difficult moments
Section 3 Heading: The 45-second loop
Section 3 Body: The full Validation and Support response can take as little as 45 seconds. It has two steps: validate first, then support. That's it. The more you practise, the more natural it becomes.
Section 4 Heading: Why Order Matters
Section 4 Body: The order of operations is essential for success. Validation -> Emotional Support -> Practical Support follows the neural requirements for moving from a state of stress/threat to a state of increased connectedness and flexibility.
Section 4 Table:
Step 1: Validation | Neural Focus: ↓ Amygdala | State: From Threat to Safe
Step 2A: Emotional Support | Neural Focus: ↑ Oxytocin (Relational) | State: From Safe to Connected
Step 2B: Practical Support | Neural Focus: ↑ Prefrontal Cortex (Cognitive) | State: From Connected to Active
Section 4 Body 2: If the order is reversed (e.g., giving advice before validation), the brain’s threat detection remains active, and the PFC never fully engages, leading to unproductive conversation and increased distress.
Section 5 Heading: Adapting the Framework
Section 5 Body: We suggest you learn the framework, then adapt its delivery depending on you're child’s personality, sensitivities, etc., including where they are at on the continuum of need:
Section 5 Bullets:
Sometimes a quick validation + 1 "because” can be enough to shift things in a meaningful direction. Other times, you might need to do a few rounds of because-statements, + emotional and practical support for your child to de-escalate and trust your efforts.
“Drive-thru” validation with just one because statement (and nothing more) might be the place to start for some kids who feel awkward with emotion-talk. Either way, our suggestion is for you to learn the framework to acquire the skill, develop fluency and the capacity for eventual discernment in its application, and that takes time and practice.

Topic 2: Knee-Jerk Reactions
Subtitle: Recognizing your go-to reactions
Section 1 Heading: What is a knee-jerk reaction?
Section 1 Body: Most caregivers have a handful of knee-jerk reactions when a child is upset. These are deeply conditioned and feel natural, but they aren't always effective and can sometimes cause more problems.
Section 2 Heading: Common knee-jerk reactions
Section 2 Body: You might recognise one or more of these in yourself:
Section 2 Bullets:
Reassuring: "You'll be fine! You're so good at this."
Problem-solving: "Let's make a plan right now."
Rationalising: "It's not as bad as it seems."
Distraction: "How about we watch a movie?"
Cheerleading: "You've got this! I believe in you."
Looking on the bright side: "At least..."
"But"-ing: "I hear you, but..."
Section 2 Callout: These reactions aren't wrong, but they work better after your child's brain is in a more flexible state.
Section 3 Heading: Why noticing matters
Section 3 Body: The first step is simply awareness. Once you notice your knee-jerk reaction, you have a choice. You can still bring your strengths in after validation opens the door.

Topic 3: Step 1: Validation
Subtitle: Helping your child feel understood
Section 1 Heading: Help Their Brain Feel Safe
Section 1 Body: When a child is upset, no matter the reason, their brain becomes activated and they can't think (as) clearly. Validation sends a simple signal: I get it, I'm not against you. A tentative, caring tone ("I can understand you might be frustrated because...") helps them begin to process what they're experiencing rather than just react to it. When they feel your genuine attempt to understand, even if you are off the mark, they are more likely to be more flexible.
Section 2 Heading: What validation really means
Section 2 Body: Validation means making an effort to understand your child's experience whether it's an emotion, a thought, an urge, a behaviour, or a state, and from their point of view.
Section 2 Callout: You're not necessarily agreeing with them. You're showing you get why it makes sense from inside of their experience. That opens the door.
Section 3 Heading: More than just feelings
Section 3 Body: Most caregivers think validation means "I validate that you're sad." But you can validate almost anything:
Section 3 Bullets:
Feelings: sad, mad, ashamed, lonely, afraid
Attitudes: "This is stupid." "There's no point."
Urges: "I want to quit." "I don't want to go."
Behaviours: outbursts, withdrawal, aggression
States: overwhelm, fatigue, silence, sensory overload
Section 4 Heading: How to build a validation sentence
Section 4 Body: Start with a sentence opener, add a verb, then three "because" statements that explain why their reaction makes sense.
Section 5 Heading: Sentence starters
Section 5 Body: Choose one that feels authentic to you:
Section 5 Bullets:
"I could understand if you…"
"I can imagine you…"
"No wonder you…"
"It makes sense that you…"
"I will never fully understand, but when I try to put myself in your shoes, I imagine you…"
"When I try to see it from your perspective, I imagine you…"
Section 6 Heading: Followed by a verb
Section 6 Body: feel / think / want / don't want to / might not want to
Section 6 Callout: Example: "I can imagine you don't want to stop playing..."
Section 7 Heading: The power of "because"
Section 7 Body: Because-statements are the heart of validation. They show your child that you've thought about their experience from the inside.
Section 7 Bullets:
Aim for 3 because-statements
End each one with a downward tone, not a question
The most powerful ones reflect the other's pain, needs, or suffering
When in doubt, use the 3 Hs: "it's hard", "it's heavy", "it hurts"
Section 7 Callout: "No wonder you feel that way because it's hard, and it's heavy, and it really hurts."

Topic 4: Step 2A: Emotional Support
Subtitle: Offering warmth and presence
Section 1 Heading: Build the Bridge
Section 1 Body: Once they're a little calmer, or at least feel like you're on the same team, they need to feel your warmth before they can receive anything else. Comfort, encouragement, reassurance and togetherness let them know they're not alone in this. Without this step, any suggestion you offer may feel like pressure, or unwelcome.
Section 2 Heading: Choose 2–3 of these
Section 2 Body: These sentences will let your child know that they are not alone.
Section 2 Bullets:
Comfort / Love - Loving words, a hug, or a hand on the shoulder
Reassurance / Instillation of hope - "It's going to be okay." / "I believe it will get easier."
Positive regard - "I know you're doing the best you can right now."
Belief in them - "I believe in you." / "I know you can do this."
Togetherness - "We're in this together." / "I want the best for you too."
Sharing a perspective - "Did you know...?" / "From my end, I..."
Space with a plan - "Why don't we take 5 and come back to it?"
Section 2 Callout: When it comes to length, follow your child's lead. Some moments call for a one or two emotional support sentences, others for longer presence.

Topic 5: Step 2B: Practical Support
Subtitle: Gently offering to help
Section 1 Heading: Getting Practical
Section 1 Body: Practical support works best once your child feels heard and connected. Offered too early, even the most helpful suggestion can feel like you're skipping over their experience and their walls can go up in an instant.
Section 2 Heading: Now They're Ready
Section 2 Body: When a child feels both understood and connected, even if just a bit, they become more open to thinking through the problem with you, or hearing what you have to say - whether that's finding a solution, trying something new, or shifting gears. Offered too soon, the same words can close things down.
Section 3 Heading: Types of practical support
Section 3 Body: Choose 1–2 of these:
Section 3 Bullets:
A behavioural plan or next step
A soothing activity (a walk, music, a movie)
A plan to reconnect or spend time together
Sharing a skill, brainstorming, or offering to take over
Naming a limit or a behavioural expectation
Section 4 Heading: Sometimes nothing more is needed
Section 4 Body: Once a child feels validated and emotionally supported, the moment may already be complete. Presence alone can be the most powerful thing you offer.
Section 4 Callout: "Right now, I can just be with you." This is a complete and valid response.

Topic 6: Questions vs. Suggestions
Subtitle: Knowing when to shift your approach
Section 1 Heading: Questions are great... and...
Section 1 Body: This tool uses suggestions (e.g. "I can imagine you might feel...") instead of questions ("How do you feel?") for a specific reason. Here are some potential benefits of using suggestions.
Section 2 Heading: Benefits for Validation
Section 2 Body: Suggestions can be helpful when a child has:
A limited vocabulary for emotions or lacks clarity about their internal experience
Clarity about their internal experience, but feels shame expressing it
Section 3 Heading: Benefits for Support
Section 3 Body: Suggestions can also be helpful for support when a child has:
A lack of clarity about what they need or feel
Clarity about needing support, but feels shame asking for it
Section 3 Callout: That said, feel free to use questions if they feel useful to you and your child.

Scenario 1: Worried About a Test

Title: Worried About a Test
Child Age: 9 years old
Emotion Tag: Anxiety
Situation: Your child comes home from school looking upset. They just found out they have a big math test tomorrow.
Child Statement: "I can't do this. I'm going to fail and everyone will laugh at me."
Knee-Jerk Reactions:
Reassuring: "You'll be fine, you're so good at math!"
Problem-solving: "Let's make a study plan right now."
Rationalising: "It's not as bad as it seems."
Distraction: "How about we watch a movie?"
Validation Starters: It sounds like, I can see that, I hear that, It makes sense that
Validation Directions: you're feeling really scared about this test, you're worried about what might happen tomorrow, this feels really overwhelming right now, you're feeling a lot of pressure
Because Statements: you want to do well, this feels really important to you, you care about how your classmates see you, tests can feel really unpredictable
Emotional Support Options:
I'm here with you, and we can get through this together.
Your feelings make complete sense to me.
It's okay to feel scared. That feeling won't last forever.
I love you, and that doesn't depend on how any test goes.
You don't have to face this alone.
Practical Support Options:
Would it help to look over the material together for a little while?
We could write down the things you do know to help you feel more ready.
Would you like to talk through what worries you most about the test?
Let's make sure you get a good rest tonight so you feel your best.

Scenario 2: Left Out by Friends

Title: Left Out by Friends
Child Age: 8 years old
Emotion Tag: Sadness
Situation: Your child comes home quieter than usual. At dinner, they tell you they weren't included in a game at recess and sat alone.
Child Statement: "Nobody likes me. I don't want to go to school anymore."
Knee-Jerk Reactions:
Reassuring: "You'll be fine, you're so good at math!"
Problem-solving: "Let's make a study plan right now."
Rationalising: "It's not as bad as it seems."
Distraction: "How about we watch a movie?"
Validation Starters: It sounds like, I can see that, I hear you saying, It makes so much sense that
Validation Directions: today felt really lonely for you, being left out really hurt, you're feeling like you don't belong right now, you're carrying a lot of sadness from today
Because Statements: being included really matters to you, you were hoping to feel connected today, sitting alone is a hard feeling to carry, friendships mean a lot to you
Emotional Support Options:
I'm so glad you told me how you're feeling.
You don't have to carry this feeling alone.
What you're feeling makes complete sense.
I love you exactly as you are.
Being left out is a really hard experience, and your hurt is real.
Practical Support Options:
Would it help to talk about what happened today?
Is there anything you'd like from me right now?
Would you like some quiet time, or would you rather be with me for a bit?
We could think together, when you're ready, about how tomorrow might go.

Scenario 3: Angry at a Sibling

Title: Angry at a Sibling
Child Age: 7 years old
Emotion Tag: Anger
Situation: Your younger child runs to you furious after their older sibling broke their favourite toy during an argument.
Child Statement: "I hate them! They always ruin everything. It's not fair!"
Knee-Jerk Reactions:
Reassuring: "You'll be fine, you're so good at math!"
Problem-solving: "Let's make a study plan right now."
Rationalising: "It's not as bad as it seems."
Distraction: "How about we watch a movie?"
Validation Starters: Ican see that, It makes sense that, It sounds like, I hear how
Validation Directions: you're feeling really angry right now, this feels really unfair to you, losing that toy really hurt, you're feeling like your things aren't respected
Because Statements: that toy was important to you, you feel like this keeps happening, fairness really matters to you, it feels like no one is on your side right now
Emotional Support Options:
Your anger makes complete sense to me.
I'm on your side, and I hear you.
You're allowed to feel angry about this.
I'm here. You don't have to calm down right away.
It's okay to have big feelings. I'm not going anywhere.
Practical Support Options:
When you're ready, we can talk about what happened.
Would it help to have some space right now, or do you want to stay with me?

Scenario 4: Scared at Bedtime

Title: Scared at Bedtime
Child Age: 6 years old
Emotion Tag: Fear
Situation: At bedtime, your child refuses to go to sleep alone and becomes increasingly distressed, saying there are monsters.
Child Statement: "There's something in my room. I'm too scared. Please don't leave me."
Knee-Jerk Reactions:
Reassuring: "You'll be fine, you're so good at math!"
Problem-solving: "Let's make a study plan right now."
Rationalising: "It's not as bad as it seems."
Distraction: "How about we watch a movie?"
Validation Starters: It sounds like, I can see that, I hear that, It makes sense that
Validation Directions: you're feeling really scared right now, bedtime feels frightening tonight, being alone in the dark feels really hard, you need to feel safe before you can rest
Because Statements: the dark can feel really big and unknown, your imagination is very active at night, you need to know someone is close, nighttime can bring up big feelings
Emotional Support Options:
I'm right here. You're not alone.
It's okay to feel scared. Your feelings are real.
I'll stay close until you feel more settled.
Your feelings make sense to me, and I'm not going anywhere.
Being scared at night is something a lot of children feel.
Practical Support Options:
Would a nightlight help you feel safer?
I can sit with you until you feel sleepy.
Would you like to keep the door open a little?
Let's think of something peaceful together to help you drift off.
